# Number sequences

A number sequence is an ordered list of numbers. Here, the first term is $u_1$
and indexing starts at $1$.

<Grid title="Notation and conventions">
  <Definition title="Notation">
    - $n$ is the **index** and $u_n$ is the value of the term at index $n$.
    - The first difference is $\Delta u_n=u_{n+1}-u_n$.
    - The second difference is $\Delta^2u_n=\Delta(\Delta u_n)$.
  </Definition>

  <Note title="Two ways to define a sequence">
    An **explicit formula** gives $u_n$ directly from $n$. A **recurrence
    relation** calculates $u_{n+1}$ from $u_n$.
  </Note>

  <Warning title="Starting-index convention">
    Some courses start at $u_0$. The formulas then become
    $u_n=u_0+nr$ and $u_n=u_0q^n$.
  </Warning>
</Grid>

<Grid title="Arithmetic and geometric sequences">
  <Definition title="Arithmetic sequence">
    The difference between consecutive terms is constant:
    $u_{n+1}-u_n=r$.
  </Definition>

  <Formula title="Arithmetic formulas">
    $$u_{n+1}=u_n+r$$
    $$u_n=u_1+(n-1)r=an+b$$
    where $a=r$ and $b=u_1-r$.
  </Formula>

  <Example title="Constant difference">
    For $3,7,11,15,19,\ldots$, $r=4$ and $u_1=3$, so
    $$u_n=3+4(n-1)=4n-1.$$
  </Example>

  <Definition title="Geometric sequence">
    The ratio of consecutive terms is constant:
    $\frac{u_{n+1}}{u_n}=q$, when $u_n\ne0$.
  </Definition>

  <Formula title="Geometric formulas">
    $$u_{n+1}=qu_n$$
    $$u_n=u_1q^{n-1}$$
  </Formula>

  <Example title="Constant ratio">
    For $3,6,12,24,48,\ldots$, $q=2$, so
    $$u_n=3\cdot2^{n-1}.$$
  </Example>
</Grid>

<Grid title="Constant differences and degree">
  <Note title="How the method works" wide>
    For a polynomial sequence, the first row of differences that becomes
    constant indicates the polynomial's degree.
  </Note>

  <DataTable title="Polynomial patterns" wide compact lines="rows">

    | Constant row | Type | General form |
    | --- | --- | --- |
    | values $u_n$ | constant, degree 0 | $u_n=c$ |
    | $\Delta u_n$ | linear, degree 1 | $u_n=an+b$ |
    | $\Delta^2u_n$ | quadratic, degree 2 | $u_n=an^2+bn+c$ |
    | $\Delta^3u_n$ | cubic, degree 3 | $u_n=an^3+bn^2+cn+d$ |
    | $\Delta^ku_n$ | polynomial, degree $k$ | $u_n=a_kn^k+\cdots+a_0$ |

  </DataTable>

  <Example title="Perfect squares">
    For $1,4,9,16,25,\ldots$, the first differences are
    $3,5,7,9,\ldots$ and the second differences are always $2$.
    The sequence is quadratic: $u_n=n^2$.
  </Example>

  <Formula title="Quadratic coefficient">
    If $u_n=an^2+bn+c$, then
    $$
    \Delta^2u_n=2a \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
    a=\frac{\Delta^2u_n}{2}.
    $$
    Two other terms can then determine $b$ and $c$.
  </Formula>

  <CommonMistake>
    The constant-difference method does not identify a geometric sequence:
    its differences do not remain constant indefinitely. Test ratios
    separately.
  </CommonMistake>
</Grid>

<Grid>
  <Summary>

  1. Look for a constant difference: it identifies a polynomial sequence.
  2. Otherwise, look for a constant ratio: it identifies a geometric sequence.
  3. An explicit formula gives $u_n$ directly; a recurrence uses earlier terms.

  </Summary>
</Grid>
